## Narration (~750 words, ~2.5 words/sec)

### 1. Hook (0:00–0:30)
Thirty-three thousand people. One city block, smaller than seven football fields. No building codes. Almost no police. And it sat in the middle of Hong Kong, one of the richest cities on Earth. Inside, dentists with no licence pulled teeth next to heroin dens. Jets roared so low over the rooftops that kids could almost touch them. So how did a place like this exist for nearly a century, and why did it vanish almost overnight?

### 2. A fort nobody owned (0:30–1:15)
It starts with a mistake. In the 1840s, Britain took Hong Kong Island after the First Opium War. China's Qing dynasty answered by building a walled fort in Kowloon, just across the harbour, to keep an eye on the British. Then in 1898, Britain leased the land all around it for ninety-nine years. But the treaty had one strange exception. The fort would stay Chinese, with Chinese officials still inside, in the middle of British territory. Within a year, the British forced those officials out. China never accepted it. So for decades, this tiny patch of land sat in a legal no-man's-land. Britain claimed it. China claimed it. Neither really governed it.

### 3. Walls down, people in (1:15–1:45)
In the Second World War, Japan occupied Hong Kong and tore down the city's stone walls to expand the nearby Kai Tak airport. The walls were gone, but the name stayed. After the war, refugees poured in from China's civil war, and they found the one place in Hong Kong where nobody could stop you from building. Shacks became houses. Houses became concrete towers. No architects. No permits. No inspections.

### 4. The Triads' city (1:45–2:30)
And when the government steps back, someone else steps in. By the 1950s and 60s, large parts of the Walled City were in the hands of the Triads, Hong Kong's organised crime gangs. Police rarely went inside, and when they did, they went in numbers. In the narrow alleys you could find opium and heroin dens, illegal gambling halls, brothels, and restaurants serving dog meat, which was banned everywhere else in Hong Kong. To outsiders, it had a simple name: the City of Darkness. But here's the part most people get wrong. For most of the people living there, it wasn't a hideout. It was just home.

### 5. How 33,000 people lived (2:30–3:45)
By the 1980s, more than three hundred buildings had fused into a single solid block, most of them around ten to fourteen storeys high. They couldn't go much higher, because planes landing at Kai Tak passed directly overhead. At its peak, around thirty-three thousand people lived here, making it one of the most densely populated places ever recorded. Sunlight barely reached the ground. The alleys were lit by fluorescent tubes day and night, and water dripped constantly from a tangle of pipes overhead. People crossed between buildings on the rooftops, where children played while jets thundered past. And yet, it worked. Small factories made fishballs, noodles and plastic toys for shops across the city. Doctors and dentists trained in mainland China, whose qualifications weren't recognised in Hong Kong, opened clinics here because nobody checked licences, and they charged a fraction of what you'd pay outside. There were schools, temples, and a post office. The mail still got delivered. Residents organised themselves to deal with rubbish, fires and disputes, because nobody else was going to. Dangerous and orderly. Criminal and close-knit. All at once.

### 6. Crackdown and the deal (3:45–4:15)
In the 1970s, police launched waves of raids, breaking the Triads' open grip on the streets. Then, in 1984, Britain and China agreed that Hong Kong would return to China in 1997. For the first time in nearly a century, both sides wanted the same thing. And in 1987, they announced it together. The Walled City would be torn down. Thirty-three thousand people would have to leave.

### 7. Demolition and today (4:15–5:00)
Some residents took the compensation and left. Others protested, saying they had built their whole lives inside those walls. Family by family, shop by shop, the buildings emptied. In 1993 the demolition began, and by 1994 the most crowded place on Earth was gone. Today, the site is a quiet park, with gardens, ponds, and pieces of the old fort dug up during demolition. But the Walled City never really disappeared. Its tangled alleys and neon signs inspired films, games and countless sci-fi cities. It was born from a gap two governments refused to fill, and the people inside filled it themselves. So how many places like it still exist today?
