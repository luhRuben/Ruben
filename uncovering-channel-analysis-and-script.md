## 2. Content pillars (scenarios)

Each video fits one of five repeatable story types:

1. **The Impossible Place.** A city, prison, border or zone that "shouldn't exist" (ghost cities, lawless enclaves, closed towns). The draw is *"how do people live there?"*
2. **The Escape / Heist.** A prison break or a large-scale crime, told as a step-by-step operation. The draw is *"how did they pull it off?"*
3. **The Disaster Autopsy.** A concert crush or infrastructure failure, told minute by minute. The draw is *"who is to blame, and how did nobody see it coming?"*
4. **The Shadow Power.** Mafias, cartels, gangs, and states inside states. The draw is *"who really runs this?"*
5. **The Hot-Zone Explainer.** A border or conflict, explained through one concrete place or person instead of abstract politics.

**What they have in common:** a real, checkable event; a clear villain or system; a mystery you can state in one sentence; and visuals that work with stock footage, archive footage, maps and satellite imagery. No face cam is needed.

---

## 3. Hook formula

The first 30 seconds follow this pattern:

| Beat | Time | What it does | Example line |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Cold open on the most extreme fact** | 0–5s | Shock plus a picture the viewer can see | *"33,000 people. One city block. No police."* |
| **2. The contradiction** | 5–12s | Why this shouldn't be possible | *"…and it was sitting in the middle of one of the richest cities on Earth."* |
| **3. Stakes / escalation** | 12–20s | 2–3 rapid, specific details | dentists with no licence, heroin dens, mail still delivered |
| **4. The open loop** | 20–30s | A question the video promises to answer | *"So how did a place like this exist for almost 100 years, and why did it vanish overnight?"* |

**Hook rules:**
- Start with a **number or a picture**, never "Hey guys" or "In this video".
- Promise an **answer** ("how", "why"), not just a list.
- Keep the title, thumbnail and first line in sync: the thumbnail shows the image, the title asks the question, and the first line gives the shocking fact.

**Title templates in this niche:**
- *The [Place] Where [Impossible Thing] Happened*
- *How [N] Men Escaped [The Most Secure Prison] in [Country]*
- *Why [Country] Can't Stop [Problem]*
- *Inside the [Superlative] [Place] on Earth*
- *What Really Happened at [Event]*

**Thumbnail pattern:** one striking real image (a dense building, a fence, a crowd) + a red circle or arrow + 2–4 words of text at most.

## 4. Retention structure (a 10–15 minute video)

1. **Hook (0:00–0:30)**: the formula above.
2. **Context fast-forward (0:30–2:00)**: how it began, in under 90 seconds with a map.
3. **Act 1, Rise (2:00–5:00)**: how it grew; first escalation.
4. **Mid-video re-hook (~5:00)**: *"But that was nothing compared to what came next."*
5. **Act 2, Peak (5:00–9:00)**: the darkest or most surprising section; specific stories about named people or numbers.
6. **Sponsor slot**: around 1:30 or after the first act, with a one-line bridge from the story.
7. **Act 3, Fall / Resolution (9:00–12:00)**: the crackdown, the escape or the collapse.
8. **Payoff + "today" (12:00–end)**: what is there now, and why it still matters.
9. **End-screen bridge**: point to the next related video ("if you thought this was crazy, watch…").

**Narration style:** second person ("you"), short sentences, present tense in dramatic moments, and a question at the end of each section to open the next loop.

## 5. Full script

**Pillar:** The Impossible Place + The Shadow Power
**Title:** *The Lawless City Where 33,000 People Lived in One Block*
**Alt titles:** *Inside Kowloon Walled City: The Most Crowded Place Ever* / *The City Hong Kong Couldn't Police*
**Thumbnail:** an aerial shot of the walled city's solid wall of buildings, a plane overhead, the text "NO LAWS"
**Target length:** ~13 minutes (~2,000 words of narration)

---

### [0:00] HOOK

**VISUAL:** A slow push into the archive aerial of Kowloon Walled City: one solid concrete block. A plane roars overhead.

**VO:**
Thirty-three thousand people.
One city block, smaller than seven football fields.
Almost no police, no building codes, and no clear government.

And it sat in the middle of Hong Kong, one of the richest cities on Earth.

Inside, you could get a tooth pulled by a dentist with no licence. Buy heroin two doors down from a kindergarten. Eat at a restaurant in a hallway that never saw the sun.

Planes passed so low overhead that residents say they could read the logos on the wings.

So how did a place like this exist for almost a century, and why did it disappear almost overnight?

**[TITLE CARD: KOWLOON WALLED CITY]**

---

### [0:35] CONTEXT: A FORT NOBODY OWNED

**VISUAL:** An animated map: China, Hong Kong Island, the Kowloon peninsula.

**VO:**
To understand the Walled City, you have to understand a mistake.

In the 1800s, Britain took Hong Kong Island after the First Opium War. China's Qing dynasty responded by turning a small coastal outpost in Kowloon into a fort. In 1847 they built thick stone walls around it, and they had a clear purpose: to keep an eye on the British.

Then in 1898 Britain leased the New Territories, a large area around Kowloon, for 99 years. The treaty had one strange exception. The fort would stay Chinese, with Chinese officials still inside, in the middle of British territory.

**VISUAL:** Zoom in on the map. A small dot stays red while everything around it turns British blue.

**VO:**
Within a year the British pushed those officials out. China never accepted that, so for the next several decades the land sat in a legal no-man's-land. Britain claimed it. China claimed it. Neither really governed it.

Remember that, because everything that follows comes from it.

---

### [2:00] ACT 1: THE WALLS COME DOWN, THE PEOPLE MOVE IN

**VISUAL:** Second World War archive footage; Japanese troops in Hong Kong.

**VO:**
In World War Two Japan occupied Hong Kong, and they wanted to expand the nearby Kai Tak airport. So they tore down the Walled City's walls and used the stone.

The name stayed, even though the walls were gone.

After the war, China fell into civil war. Hundreds of thousands of refugees poured south into Hong Kong. They needed somewhere to live, and there was one patch of land where nobody could stop you from building.

**VISUAL:** Photos of shacks becoming low concrete buildings.

**VO:**
First came shacks. Then two-storey houses. Then concrete towers, put up with no architects, no permits and no inspections, wherever there was space.

When the British tried to clear the area in 1948, there were riots. The political cost was too high, so Hong Kong mostly backed off.

And when the government backs off, somebody else moves in.

---

### [3:30] SPONSOR SLOT

**VO:**
*(Bridge)* Speaking of places nobody is watching, your personal data is one of them, and that's where today's sponsor comes in…
*[Insert a 45–60 second read]*
Now, back to the city with no rules.

---

### [4:30] ACT 2: THE TRIADS' CITY

**VISUAL:** Dark alleys, dripping pipes, neon signs (archive photos with a slow Ken Burns move).

**VO:**
By the 1950s and 60s, the Walled City was largely in the hands of the Triads, Hong Kong's organised crime groups.

Police rarely went in, and when they did, they went in large groups.

Inside the alleys you could find opium and later heroin dens, illegal gambling, brothels, and food stalls that served dog meat, which was banned in the rest of Hong Kong.

**[MID-VIDEO RE-HOOK, ~5:00]**

**VO:**
But here is the part most people get wrong about Kowloon Walled City.

It wasn't only a criminal hideout. For most of the people inside, it was simply home.

---

### [5:15] ACT 2 (cont.): HOW 33,000 PEOPLE ACTUALLY LIVED

**VISUAL:** A 3D cutaway animation of the buildings, with labels appearing floor by floor.

**VO:**
By the 1980s, more than 300 buildings had grown into each other, forming what looked like one solid block. Most were around 10 to 14 storeys. They couldn't go much higher, because the planes landing at Kai Tak passed right overhead.

At its peak, an estimated 33,000 people lived here. That made it one of the most densely populated places in recorded history, around a million people per square kilometre.

Sunlight barely reached the ground level, so the alleys stayed lit by fluorescent tubes day and night. Water dripped constantly from hundreds of pipes running along the ceilings. Residents walked between buildings across the rooftops, and kids played up there while jets roared over their heads.

**VISUAL:** Rooftop photos: TV antennas, kids, a plane passing low.

**VO:**
And it worked, in its own way.

Small factories made fishballs, plastic toys and noodles, often for shops in the rest of Hong Kong. Doctors and dentists trained in mainland China, whose qualifications weren't recognised in Hong Kong, opened clinics here, because nobody checked licences. Their prices were a fraction of what you paid outside.

There were schools, temples and a post office. Mail was delivered.

Residents formed their own associations to deal with problems like rubbish and fires, because no one else was going to.

**VO:**
So you had a place that was dangerous and orderly, criminal and close-knit, all at once.

But Hong Kong was changing, and the Walled City's time was running out.

---

### [8:00] ACT 3: THE CRACKDOWN

**VISUAL:** Police archive footage from the 1970s, raids and headlines.

**VO:**
In the 1970s, Hong Kong police launched a wave of raids into the Walled City. They made arrests, seized drugs, and broke the Triads' open control of the streets.

Crime didn't vanish, but the Walled City stopped being the lawless fortress of the 50s. What was left was something just as hard to deal with: tens of thousands of people living in conditions most of the world would call impossible.

**VISUAL:** Map animation: 1984 headline "Sino-British Joint Declaration".

**VO:**
Then, in 1984, Britain and China agreed that Hong Kong would return to China in 1997.

That removed the old problem. For the first time in nearly a century, both sides wanted the same thing.

And in 1987, they announced it together: the Walled City would be torn down.

---

### [9:30] ACT 3 (cont.): CLEARING 33,000 PEOPLE

**VISUAL:** Photos of residents protesting, then empty corridors.

**VO:**
Imagine telling an entire town that it has to leave.

The government spent billions of Hong Kong dollars on compensation and resettlement. Some residents took the money and left. Others fought it, saying they'd built their lives and businesses here and the offers weren't fair.

Over the next few years the buildings emptied, family by family, shop by shop.

**VISUAL:** Demolition footage, 1993; machines biting into the concrete mass.

**VO:**
In 1993 the demolition began. By 1994, the most crowded place on Earth was gone.

---

### [11:00] PAYOFF: WHAT'S THERE TODAY

**VISUAL:** Present-day footage of Kowloon Walled City Park: ponds, gardens, quiet paths.

**VO:**
Today, if you go to the spot, you'll find a park.

Quiet paths. Chinese gardens. A few pieces of the original fort that were dug up during demolition, including the old stone South Gate and the yamen, the official building from the Qing days.

There's almost nothing to tell you that 33,000 people once lived stacked on top of each other here.

**VO:**
But the Walled City didn't disappear completely. It lives on in films, video games and comics. Its look became shorthand for a crowded, lawless future city, and if you've ever seen a sci-fi city full of tangled cables and neon alleys, there's a good chance it was inspired by this place.

---

### [12:15] CLOSING THOUGHT + END SCREEN

**VISUAL:** Slow fade from the park back to the archive aerial.

**VO:**
Kowloon Walled City wasn't created by the people who lived there. It was created by a gap, a small patch of land two governments argued over and neither would take responsibility for.

The people inside filled that gap the only way they could: with each other.

And it raises a question worth asking. How many places like this still exist today, places where the official rules stop and something else takes over?

**VISUAL:** End screen with the next video.

**VO:**
One of them is a city in [insert next video's topic], and it might be even stranger than this one. You can watch that story right here.

## 6. Production notes

- **Fact-check before recording.** Population (33,000 is the commonly cited 1987 figure), density, building count, raid details and the compensation total all vary by source. Check each against at least two sources, such as the Hong Kong government, Greg Girard and Ian Lambot's *City of Darkness*, and SCMP archives, and adjust the wording.
- **Footage.** Use archive photos that are licensed or used under fair use, Google Earth / map animations, a 3D cutaway (Blender or an AI image-to-video tool), and present-day park B-roll.
- **Shorts / TikTok cuts:** (1) the hook, ending on "watch the full story"; (2) "The unlicensed dentists of Kowloon"; (3) "The planes that flew over 33,000 people's roofs".
- **Next videos in the same pillar,** for the end screen: Hashima "Battleship" Island, Japan; Christiania, Copenhagen; the Tower of David, Caracas; Nicosia's UN buffer zone; Kolmanskop, Namibia.
